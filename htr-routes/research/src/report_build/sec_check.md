## 4. The checklists, subset by subset

This is the main table: a checklist for every subset × edges, because the bad-edge count changes which routes are good, not only the corner subset (as a solver pointed out; how much is measured below). For each subset × edges: the first three routes of the budget-2 checklist, the share of DRs where the routes so far find an optimal finish after 1, 2, 3 and 5 routes, and the share where the same three find a finish within +1. Sampled, {= n(25000) =} states per subset × edges; brackets are the part after the NISS switch; a route ends at HTR (`0c0`). All 8 routes with their cumulative shares at the optimum and at +1, and the no-limit lists, are in appendix A.

Averaged over all DRs (each subset × edges by its real frequency), the own list finds the optimal on {= EGA('opt', 1) =} percentage points more of DRs after one route, {= EGA('opt', 2) =} after two, {= EGA('opt', 3) =} after three and {= EGA('opt', 5) =} after five; within +1, {= EGA('p1', 1) =} and {= EGA('p1', 3) =} points after one and three routes. *Pooled first route: rank in own order* is where the pooled list's first route lands in the subset × edges list (computed to 60 routes). How to read a row, with 4c3 (the pooled row; 4c3 2e and 4e start the same way): try `4a3 > 4b2 > (4a1 > 0c0)` first: on the 4a3 side, one qt to 4b2, switch, and on the other side 4a1 and HTR. The same moves read the other way round are its mirror, `4b3 > 4a2 > 4a1 > (0c0)`: starting on the 4b3 side, qt to 4a2, qt to 4a1, switch for the last qt. Every split of a finish has such a mirror, and the table names both; as classes they are not the same (each also collects other finishes, and only {= n(E['alias_pairs'][1]) =} of {= n(E['alias_pairs'][0]) =} mirror pairs cover exactly the same states), so the share is for the reading named first. With HTRs up to two over the shortest that route alone finds the optimal on {= BUD('4c3', '2', 1) =} of 4c3. Then the b side: `4b3 > 2c4 > 2c3 > (4b2 > 4a1 > 0c0)`, then the regression through 2c3 on the a side. The b-side route through 4a2 read linearly, `4b3 > 4a2 > 4a1 > 0c0`, is fifth. An example is in the [worked examples](examples.html) and this one: the 4c3 4e reached by `{= WIT('W01', 'scramble') =}` is {= WIT('W01', 'optimal') =} optimal, and the first route finds it as `{= WIT('W01', 'normal_moves') =}` on normal, then `({= WIT('W01', 'inverse_moves') =})` on inverse, HTR in {= WIT('W01', 'htr_length') =} (replayed).

What the pooled table says, subset by subset, in words:

- **0c3, 0c4, 2c4, 2c5, 4b5** are the easy ones for a checklist: three routes cover {= BUD('0c3', '2', 3) =}, {= BUD('0c4', '2', 3) =}, {= BUD('2c4', '2', 3) =}, {= BUD('2c5', '2', 3) =} and {= BUD('4b5', '2', 3) =}. 0c3: `4a2 4e` then switch. 0c4: `4a3 > 4b2`, switch. 2c4, 2c5, 4b5: down the 2c chain to 2c3, switch at 2c3 (or one step earlier), or linear. This is what Daichi's cheat sheet says.
- **4c3, 4c4, 2c3** need four or five routes for 80% ({= BUD('4c3', '2', 5) =}, {= BUD('4c4', '2', 5) =}, {= BUD('2c3', '2', 5) =} after five); the first one does most of the work.
- **4b2, 4a1, 0c0** are where checklists struggle: the optimal is spread over many routes, and eight routes stop at {= BUD('0c0', '2', 8) =} (0c0), {= BUD('4a1', '2', 8) =} (4a1), {= BUD('4b2', '2', 8) =} (4b2). These are the subsets where bounding does better (section 8). On 4b2 the 2qt route `4b2 > 4a1 > 0c0` comes first but alone finds only {= BUD('4b2', '2', 1) =}; the regressions through 2c3 come next.
- **Within +1** the same three routes reach {= ', '.join(f"{pc(SAMEF(g, '2', 3))} on {g}" for g in G12 if SAMEF(g, '2', 3) < 0.9) =}, and 90% or more on the other {= sum(1 for g in G12 if SAMEF(g, '2', 3) >= 0.9) =}. If you only need a finish within one move of optimal, three routes are enough nearly everywhere. (At +1 a route counts if any of its finishes within +1 has an HTR within the budget, which may be shorter than on its optimal finishes.)

**Cheap and marginal routes.** After the third route, each further route adds at most {= f1(E['marg_after3_max'] * 100) =} points, and in {= pc(E['marg_after3_share_lt3']) =} of the checklists (subsets × edges and corner subsets) less than 3 points (budget 2). **Do edge paths make a better checklist?** No. An edge path lies inside its corner route, so a list of edge paths finds less per route; what it shows is how far a corner route is spread over edge paths. Built the same way (greedy, budget 2, per subset × edges), the first edge path finds the optimal on {= ESA('opt', 'edge', 0) =} of all DRs against {= ESA('opt', 'corner', 0) =} for the first corner route, three edge paths on {= ESA('opt', 'edge', 2) =} against {= ESA('opt', 'corner', 2) =}. The first edge path is a piece of the first corner route (or its mirror) on {= E['es']['all']['opt']['n_same_first'] =} of {= E['es']['n'] =} subset × edges (not on {= ', '.join(ESL(lambda v: not v['first_same_corner'])) =}), so it does not change what to try. One edge path already finds everything the first corner route finds on {= ESM(1) =} subset × edges ({= ', '.join(ESL(lambda v: v['match1'] == 1)) =}): there the corner route effectively has one edge path. It takes two on {= ESM(2) =}, and up to eight on {= ', '.join(ESL(lambda v: v['match1'] == 8)) =}, where the first corner route is spread over many edge paths and aiming for one edge count at each step would cost you most of it: check the corner route and take whatever edge count you land on. The edge-path checklists themselves, eight routes per subset × edges, are in appendix A, **computer only**: an answer key, not a list to check in an attempt.
