HTR cases

Every HTR case of a DR on U/D, up to rotations, mirrors and the E slice: 1,272 cases, grouped by subset, then bad edges, then the optimal: the fewest moves to HTR, leaving DR on the way or not. On each cube the bad pieces are solid gray and the rest is glass. Click a case to open it in the side panel, with its HTRs up to 0 to 4 moves over its optimal (a slider there sets it). Drag a cube there to turn it.

What counts as a case, and how the HTRs are listed
  • Bad pieces. A corner is bad when it sits in the other tetrad (where no half turn can take it); an edge is bad when an M-slice edge (UF UB DF DB) sits on an S-slice place (UL UR DL DR), or the other way. They are counted in the frame with few of them, as subsets are named: 2c3 2e and 6c3 6e are one case.
  • One case. Everything that decides which move sequences take a DR to HTR: two DRs are the same case when exactly the same sequences take them to HTR. Rotations and mirrors that keep U/D on top and bottom count as the same case (the picture shows one of them), and so does the E slice: turning it makes every bad piece good and every good one bad (2c3 2e becomes 6c3 6e). With DR moves both are the same number of moves from HTR, and they have the same HTRs, with the last quarter turn on the other layer (U for D, D for U). HTRs that leave DR on the way can tell them apart: for 25 cases the version drawn has a different optimal from the other (by one move, two for cases 674 and 732), which is what this page shows. Each case is drawn the way with fewer bad pieces: 2c rather than 6c, 0c rather than 8c, and with 4 bad corners either way, the fewer bad edges. Case 1 is the one exception: HTR with the E slice a quarter turn off, every piece bad, 2 moves (U D) from HTR, while turning the slice back makes it HTR itself.
  • Lookalikes. Several cases have the same bad pieces: they look the same here and on the cube. Each case lists its lookalikes. Every 4b2, 2c3, 2c4 and 4a3 case also carries the label solvers tell such lookalikes apart by:
    • 4b2: no parity / parity. Set the corners up for a two-quarter-turn trigger (Q X2 Q) with half turns, counting every U2 or D2 done while the bad corners are split 3/1 between U and D. An even count and the trigger gives real HTR: no parity. An odd count: parity.
    • 2c3: clockwise / anticlockwise. From the position shown, the quarter turn (U or D, each seen from its own face) that brings it to 4b2 with no parity. Every half turn done before it flips the direction.
    • 2c4 and 4a3: good / bad. A sequence progresses when it brings the corners a quarter turn closer to HTR, half turns counting nothing: the subset's number goes down. A 2c4 is good when, with its bad corners on UFR and UFL, U and B2 U both progress; on UBL and DFR, F2 U and R2 U; stacked on UFR and DFR, U2 R2 U and U2 F2 U. Then both lead to 2c3; on a bad 2c4 one leads to 2c3 and the other to 2c5. A 4a3 is good when, with its bad corners as bars on U and D, U progresses (it leads to 4b2, where a bad one's leads to 4b4); all on U, F2 U. A 4a3 with its bad corners on diagonals of U and D is neither. U or U′ makes no difference here, and the rules give one answer from every side they can be read from.
    The 4b2 rule is the one in the Hitchhiker's Guide to HTR (Yurii Riabov, July 2024, "How to solve 4b2"). The 2c3 one puts together the guide's "How to solve 2c3" (with parity after the quarter turn, turn the other way) and count quackula's "every half turn you do on a 2c3 changes the qt direction" (3 Sep 2026). Both give one answer on every route, the guide's own examples come out as it says, and the two 4b2 lookalikes of each shape are one with parity and one without. Good and bad are the rules solvers give for 2c4 and 4a3, read as above.
  • Pairs a U2 apart. Some cases are another case after U2, and on both a shortest HTR starts with a U quarter turn: U on one is U′ on the other (U2 U′ is U), so after that turn they are the same case. There are 185 such pairs, like cases 310 and 311. Each case names its pair, and the switch above the list shows every pair as one entry (1,087 entries instead of 1,272). The symmetries swap U and D, so pairs a D2 apart are among them.
  • The HTRs listed. Any face turns: the DR moves (U, D, R2, L2, F2, B2), and quarter turns of R, L, F and B, which leave DR on the way. For 155 cases such an HTR is shorter than any with DR moves (case 675 is 8 moves from HTR with DR moves, 7 with F U B′ U2 F U′ B). Turns of opposite faces go in the order U D, R L, F B, and none is in HTR before its end, even with the E slice a quarter turn off (from there the rest only turns the slice back), or has a half turn among its last turns (that half turn could go last, where it does nothing for HTR). So each one ends in a quarter turn that can go either way (X and X′ both reach HTR), and only the clockwise one is written. HTRs that are the same up to the E slice are written once, the shortest of them: with the E slice ignored, a D turn is the U turn the same way with the cube turned a quarter with it, so the turns after it are on the next faces round (R to F, F to L, L to B, B to R). Case 310's U R2 U R2 U, U R2 D B2 D, D B2 U B2 D and D B2 D L2 U are one HTR. At the panel's default, 2 moves over the optimal, that leaves 307,628 over all the cases, 121,345 of them leaving DR. Case 1, HTR up to the E slice, lists only U D. The panel lists a case's HTRs up to 0 to 4 moves over its optimal, 2 unless you change it, and never past 11 moves (a case whose optimal is longer lists its optimal ones): a 7-optimal case has about 100,000 HTRs of 11 moves.
  • For other programs. htr-cases.txt: a shortest generator of every case (moves that make it from a solved cube, as drawn; for some cases they leave DR on the way), with its subset, edges, optimal (and the optimal with DR moves only), label and bad pieces, numbered as here.
